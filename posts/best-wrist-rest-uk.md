# Best Wrist Rest UK 2026

Spend a working day at the keyboard and the small things start to tell: the spot where the heels of your hands meet the hard front edge of the desk, the pad of the palm resting on nothing in the pauses between sentences. A wrist rest is the cheap answer to that, a soft bar or pad that fills the step down from the desk edge so your hands have somewhere to settle. The snag is that "wrist rest" covers three quite different objects, and the thin round-ups lump them together and rank a shelf of near-identical pads by how many people bought one. The question that actually settles it is simpler: what are you resting? A keyboard rest, a matched set for both hands, or a mouse rest. Answer that first, glance at the fill and the base, and one clear pick is left in each camp.

## Start with what you rest, not the brand

Work out which hand is doing the complaining before you look at a single product, because the three types barely overlap.

A **keyboard rest** is the long bar that lies across the front of the board. It is what most people picture: one piece, sized to sit under both hands as you type, so the palms land on foam instead of the desk lip. If it is your typing hands that tire, this is the default.

A **matched set** adds a second, smaller pad for the mouse hand and usually a round coaster to go with it. It is the kit-the-whole-desk answer: both hands get a cushion, bought once and in one style, for roughly the price of a single good keyboard rest.

A **mouse rest** is a mouse mat with the wrist cushion built into its near edge, so the hand that works the mouse has a soft landing of its own. On a desk where the keyboard is fine but the mouse hand is the tired one, which is how a lot of spreadsheet and design days run, this is the piece to add and nothing more.

So picture your own desk. Both hands flat on the board for hours? The keyboard bar is the plain answer. Setting a desk up from scratch, or want the mouse side handled too? The set earns its keep. Only the mouse hand bothering you? The pad with a built-in rest does that one job and leaves the rest of the desk alone.

## Gel or memory foam: what the fill changes

The fill is the choice the thin round-ups skip, and it is a real one. Plain memory foam moulds to the shape of your hand for a soft, sink-in feel, but foam on its own holds heat, so it can turn warm under the wrist over a long stretch. Gel-infused memory foam, which the HyperX bar and the Gimars set both use, runs cooler than plain foam because the gel draws heat away while keeping the mould-to-your-hand give. A straight-gel pad like the Kensington is firmer and cooler still, with less sink, which some people find steadier and others find too hard. There is no right answer: warm hands and a liking for a cool, firm surface point to gel, while a softer, moulded feel points to memory foam. Both memory-foam picks here add gel precisely to keep the warm-foam problem in check.

## Size and a base that stays put

Two practical things decide whether a wrist rest is a pleasure or an irritation, and neither is the headline. The first is length. A keyboard rest wants to match the width of your board: the HyperX pad is 45.7 cm, cut for a full-size keyboard, so on a compact or tenkeyless board it hangs off both ends, and on a full-size board a shorter pad leaves a hand unsupported. Measure the front edge of your keyboard before you buy. The second is the base. A rest that slides forward every time your hands brush it is more nuisance than help, so the grip underneath matters as much as the cushion on top: the HyperX sits on anti-slip natural rubber, and the Gimars and Kensington each use non-slip bases of their own. If a listing is vague about what stops it wandering, treat that as a mark against it.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I want a keyboard rest, a mouse rest, or both?**
A: Follow the tired hand. Typing hands that want a cushion point to a keyboard rest like the HyperX, the single piece to add. For the mouse hand, a mouse pad with a built-in rest like the Kensington handles that side. Covering both, or setting a desk up from scratch, a matched set like the Gimars does the lot in one buy.

**Q: Gel or memory foam - which should I pick?**
A: Neither is better outright; they feel different. Memory foam moulds and feels soft but holds warmth; gel is firmer and runs cooler. Gel-infused memory foam, which two of our picks use, splits the difference by keeping the soft mould while shedding some heat. If your hands tend to run hot, lean gel; if you want the pad to give and mould under the wrist, lean memory foam.

**Q: Does the size of the wrist rest matter?**
A: For a keyboard rest, yes. It wants to match your board: the HyperX is 45.7 cm for a full-size keyboard, so it overhangs a compact or tenkeyless board, and a pad shorter than your keyboard leaves a hand unsupported. Measure the front edge of your keyboard first. A mouse rest is less fussy, since it only has to suit the one hand.

**Q: Will a wrist rest sort out sore wrists?**
A: We will not tell you it will. A wrist rest is a comfort item, a softer place to settle your hands between typing, and nothing on this page is a health claim. If your wrists or hands are genuinely bothering you, speak to a GP or physiotherapist rather than relying on a pad. What a good rest offers is comfort over a long session, not a remedy.

**Q: Where should a wrist rest actually sit?**
A: In front of the keyboard or mouse, so the heels of your hands settle on it in the pauses rather than under the keys, where it would lift your hands at an angle. Many people rest the palms lightly rather than bearing down the whole time.
