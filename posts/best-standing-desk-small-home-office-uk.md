## Start with the wall, not the desk

Measure the space the desk has to live in before you compare a single model. Take the width of the wall or alcove, then subtract what you need to pull the chair out and still get past it, and that smaller number is the widest desk that actually fits, not the wall measurement itself. Do the same for depth: a desk pushed against a wall still needs its feet to clear the skirting board, and a deep top can leave the chair blocking a doorway. Then look at the feet rather than the top, because the frame's feet usually run wider and further back than the desktop above them, and it is the feet, not the surface, that decide whether the desk clears a radiator or a chimney breast. Only once you have those numbers does a spec sheet mean anything: a desk that will not fit the room is not a bargain at any motor count. Height is its own separate question, and once the desk is in place our guide on [what height a standing desk should be](standing-desk-height-explained.html) walks through setting it to your own measurements rather than a number off the box.

## Will it wobble at standing height?

The spec no listing prints is the one that decides whether you keep raising the desk: how steady it is at full height. A desk that shivers when you type standing is a desk you quietly stop lifting, and then you have paid for a sitting desk. You cannot test it through a screen, but a few things on the listing stand in for it. Three-stage legs, which telescope in three sections rather than two, stay firmer at their tallest because less of the leg is extended, which is why the frame pick here is a three-stage design. A wider foot resists rocking better than a slim one, and a heavier frame simply has more metal holding it down, so a desk that quotes a serious weight is usually the steadier one. None of these is a guarantee, but read together they separate a frame that stays put from one that sways.

## One-piece top, or a frame and your own board

There are two honest ways to top a small-room desk, and they pull in opposite directions. A one-piece top, like the E1's, has no join across the surface, which reads cleaner up close where a small desk always sits, and it is the no-decisions option because the size is chosen for you. The frame-and-board route gives that decision back: you fit a top cut to the exact width of the alcove, which in a genuinely awkward space is the only way to fill the wall without a gap or an overhang. What you are weighing is finish and simplicity against a precise fit. If your wall is a standard sort of width, the one-piece desk is the easier life; if it is not, the frame earns its extra effort by fitting the room rather than the room having to accommodate the desk. A watch-out on the middle ground: some ready-made desks use a two-piece splice top, and in a small, close, well-lit room that seam shows more than it would across the room, so it is worth checking whether a top is one board or two.

## Motor noise, memory presets and the habit of standing

Two smaller things decide whether you live with a standing desk happily. The first is motor noise, which in a terraced house or a flat is a neighbour question as much as a personal one: a desk that groans every time it moves is one you raise less often when someone is on a call through the party wall. The second is memory presets. A desk with saved height buttons returns to your exact sitting and standing positions at a tap, and one without them means nudging it back into place by eye each time, which is the small friction that ends with the desk parked at one height for good. Neither shows up in a headline spec, but between them they are what turns a height-adjustable desk into one you actually adjust. ## FAQ

**Q: What is the smallest standing desk that is still worth buying?**
A: The one that fits your wall with room to move a chair, rather than the one with the smallest number in the title. Measure the usable width first, subtract the space to pull the chair out and get past, and buy the widest desk under that figure. A complete one-piece desk is the simplest way to hit a tight width without sourcing a top.

**Q: Should I buy a whole desk or a frame and my own top?**
A: Buy a whole desk if your wall is a fairly standard width, because it is less work and less chance of a sizing mistake. Choose a frame and your own board when the space is an awkward width and a stock desktop would leave a gap or overhang, since a cut-to-fit top is the only way to fill that wall properly.

**Q: How do I know a desk will not wobble when I stand?**
A: You cannot test it through a listing, but three-stage legs, a wider foot and a heavier frame all point to a steadier desk at full height. Read them together rather than trusting any one, and treat a frame that quotes a serious weight as a good sign rather than a drawback.

**Q: Do I need a dual-motor desk in a small room?**
A: Dual motors lift more evenly under an uneven load, such as a monitor on one side and nothing on the other, which is common on a small desk. It is a reasonable thing to want, but the width that fits your room matters more than the motor count, so settle the size first and treat the motors as a tie-breaker.

**Q: What height should I actually set it to?**
A: Set it to your own body rather than a number on the box, in both sitting and standing positions, and save each to a memory preset if the desk has them.
