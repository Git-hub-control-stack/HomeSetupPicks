# What The Tilt Tension Knob On An Office Chair Does

Most office chairs lean back, and most people never touch the one control that decides how that lean feels. The tilt tension knob, a dial you will find underneath the seat toward the front edge, sets how much effort it takes to recline and how firmly the chair pushes back as you do. Set it wrong and the chair either tips away the moment you relax or sits stiff however hard you lean. Set it to suit you and the recline becomes something you stop noticing, which is the point. This page covers what the knob does, why it is not the same as the tilt lock beside it, and the handful of mechanism types that decide how your particular chair reclines.

## The knob under the seat

The tilt tension control is almost always a large round knob under the seat, toward the front. SIHOO, whose M57 is the top pick in our chair guide, describes it plainly: a "tilt tension control that allows you to adjust the resistance of the tilting motion," one you "set to match your weight and preferences." Winding it one way tightens the internal spring so the chair resists recline; winding it back eases that spring so the chair leans with less push. Chair maker Merryfair puts the tuning in numbers: the tension knob "sets how hard the chair pushes back when you recline," and if you are "heavier than about 90kg" you "tighten in half-turns until the chair holds you upright at rest," while "lighter than about 60kg" you "loosen until a shoulder lean starts the recline." Matching the knob to your weight is the whole of its job, and it is why two people can call the same chair stiff and loose on the same afternoon.

## Tension sets the push, the lock holds the angle

The control next to the tension knob, often a small lever, is the tilt lock, and the two get confused constantly. Tension changes how hard it is to recline; the lock decides whether the chair reclines at all. As SIHOO puts it, a tilt lock "enables you to lock the chair in a specific position," fixing the backrest at the angle you are sitting so it stops rocking. You might run a firm tension for typing, flick the lock on to hold a slight lean through a call, then release it to rock freely again. Neither setting touches the other: a locked chair still carries whatever tension you dialled in, waiting for the moment you unlock it. If the chair rocks when you want it still, the lock is the control you want; if it rocks too easily or too stiffly once unlocked, the tension is.

## Four ways a chair actually reclines

The knob sits on top of a mechanism, and which mechanism your chair has decides how the recline moves. Four kinds are common. A free-floating tilt, in SIHOO's words, "allows the chair to tilt freely in any direction without locking," the simplest and loosest feel. A centre tilt, also called single-point, "positions the pivot directly below the middle of the seat," so the backrest and seat "move together at a 1:1 ratio" and, as Merryfair notes, "the seat pan rises in front" and your feet come up off the floor as you lean. A knee tilt moves that pivot forward: Merryfair describes it as relocating the pivot "to just behind the knees, near the front of the seat," so "the bulk of your body weight stays behind the pivot," the recline "feels smoother," and "your feet stay planted." A synchro tilt links the two parts at different rates, which Merryfair gives as a "2:1 ratio," meaning "for every 10 degrees the backrest reclines, the seat pan tilts only 5 degrees," keeping the movement measured. Knowing which one you are turning the knob on explains why the same tension setting feels different from chair to chair.

## Setting yours, and when there is nothing to set

You do not need the figures to get this right, because the chair tells you. Sit the way you normally would and lean. If the backrest gives way and you feel yourself tipping, the resistance is too low, so wind the knob clockwise a half-turn at a time and lean again after each, until the chair carries you back up on its own rather than dropping from under you. If leaning feels like pushing a wall, go the other way until a relaxed lean starts the movement. A good setting sits you steady when you relax and reclines with a gentle push, with no snapping back and no sinking. ## FAQ

**Q: Why does my office chair tip back too easily when I lean?**
A: The tilt tension is set too low for your weight, so the backrest gives way under less force than it takes to hold you. Find the round knob under the front of the seat and nudge it clockwise in small steps, leaning back after each one, until the chair sits firm when you relax and reclines only when you mean to. If tightening it as far as it goes still leaves it loose, the spring or the mechanism may be worn rather than just slack.

**Q: What is the difference between tilt tension and tilt lock?**
A: One sets the effort, the other sets the angle. Tilt tension is the resistance you feel as you lean back, changed at the knob; the tilt lock pins the backrest wherever you stop so it holds that recline rather than rocking. They work independently, so a chair you have locked still keeps whatever tension you dialled in for when you release it.

**Q: Which tilt mechanism should I look for?**
A: It depends on how you like to move rather than on a quality ranking. A knee tilt keeps your feet on the floor and reclines smoothly, which suits a full seated day; a synchro tilt moves seat and back together at a set ratio and usually pairs with a lock; a centre tilt is the simplest and lifts your feet as you lean. None of them adjusts the way the tension knob does, so the mechanism is a fixed property of the chair you pick, and our [office chair guide](best-office-chair-under-300-uk.html) notes how each one reclines.

**Q: Can every office chair's tension be adjusted?**
A: No. The tension knob is common on mid-range and ergonomic chairs but often left off cheaper ones, which use a fixed tilt with no way to change the resistance. If you cannot find a knob under the seat, check the chair's spec sheet before assuming it is missing, and if an adjustable recline matters to you, make it one of the things you confirm before you buy.
