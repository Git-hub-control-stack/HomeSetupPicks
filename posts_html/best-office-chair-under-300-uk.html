Two people with the same budget can want opposite things from a work chair. One wants mesh, to stay cool through the day; the other wants padding, to sink into something soft the moment they sit. Neither is wrong, and it is that fork, not the badge on the backrest, that this guide sorts out. Mesh lets air through, so it does not hold the warmth of the room; foam and a padded seat feel softer at once, and they are kinder if you tend to perch on the edge rather than settle right back. So the three chairs here are not first, second and third: they answer three different sitters, a fully adjustable mesh chair for the person at the desk all day, a compact one that gets out from underfoot in a small room, and a padded chair for anyone who never took to mesh. Work out which of those three is you, and the rest of this guide falls into place.

Mesh or padded: settle this first

This is the fork the whole choice turns on, so answer it before you compare anything else. Mesh is a panel of tensioned fabric, so it lets air pass straight through and stays closer to room temperature through a long session; it also pushes back a little, which some people read as support and others read as firm. Padding is foam under fabric or PU, so it gives when you sit and feels softer at once, at the cost of holding warmth against you over time. There is one detail the listings blur that is worth checking: plenty of chairs sold as mesh have a mesh back over a foam seat, which is a different thing from an all-mesh chair like the M57. If the seat is what you care about keeping cool, read the description for what the seat itself is made of, not just the back. Neither material is the right answer on its own; the right answer is the one that matches how warm you run and whether you like to sit into a chair or on top of one.

The chair has to fit the desk and the room

A chair that suits you can still be wrong for the space, so measure the room as well as yourself. The figure that catches people out is the footprint when the chair is pushed back and not in use: a wide chair with fixed arms sits out in the room and can block the route past the desk, which is exactly the problem the flip-up arms on the compact pick solve by letting it slide under. Check the seat height range against your desk too, because a chair set too high leaves your feet dangling and your forearms reaching down, and if the chair cannot come down far enough a footrest is the usual fix. Armrests are the other clearance question: arms that do not drop or fold can stop a chair tucking under a desk with a low apron or a keyboard tray. What actually adjusts, and in what order

Listings sell chairs on the number of adjustments, but the count matters less than which ones you get, so here is the order they earn their place. Seat height comes first, because a seat at the wrong height throws off everything above it, and every chair here adjusts it. Armrest height is next: arms at the right height give your forearms somewhere to rest, and arms that also fold or drop decide whether the chair tucks away. After that comes the lumbar, and this is where the chairs separate, because a support that slides up and down and in and out (as on the M57) can be set to your back, while a fixed pad or a cushion at one height is a take-it-or-leave-it affair. Tilt and recline come next, useful for shifting position through the day rather than sitting locked upright. Seat depth adjustment is the rarest at this price and the one most people can live without. Read a spec sheet in that order and a long list of features sorts quickly into the ones that will change how the chair feels and the ones that just fill a bullet list.

FAQ

Q: Is a mesh or a padded office chair better for working from home? A: Neither is better outright; they suit different people. Mesh lets air through and stays cooler over a long day, and pushes back a little under you. Padding feels softer the moment you sit and holds more warmth. Decide by how warm you run and whether you like to sit into a chair or perch on top of one, and check whether a chair sold as mesh has a mesh seat or only a mesh back over foam.

Q: What is the best office chair for a small room? A: The one that gets out of the way when you are not in it. Look for flip-up or drop-down armrests and a compact frame, so the chair slides under the desk rather than sitting out in the room, and check the seat height comes low enough to tuck away. The compact SONGMICS here is built around exactly that.

Q: Which adjustments on an office chair actually matter? A: In rough order: seat height first, then armrest height, then the lumbar, then tilt or recline, with seat depth the least essential at this price. A support you can move to your back beats a longer list of features you never touch, so weigh which adjustments you get rather than how many.

Q: Can you get a good office chair under 300 pounds? A: Yes. At this price you can buy a chair that adjusts well and sits on a solid base, like the fully adjustable mesh pick here. What the money does not stretch to is a warranty of more than a decade or replaceable parts you would repair rather than replace, so treat one of these as a chair for several good years rather than a lifetime.

Q: How do I stop the chair sitting too high for my desk? A: Lower the seat until your forearms rest level with the desk and your feet sit flat, and if the chair will not drop far enough, add a footrest to bring your feet up instead. It is the same fit question as setting the desk itself, which the standing desk guide works through from the desk's side.
