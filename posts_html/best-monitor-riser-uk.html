When a monitor sits too low on the desk, a riser is the cheapest way to lift it, and it buys back the space beneath the screen for your keyboard at the same time. First decide the material, then the model

Settle the material question first, because wood, metal and glass each lead somewhere quite different.

A wooden riser is the warm, dependable default. It is usually a fixed-height shelf, often with a second tier for a little extra room, and it looks at home on a desk made of wood or anything close to it. You do not adjust it; you set the screen on top and leave it. For most people at most desks, this is the one to picture first.

A metal riser trades the warm look for mechanism. The useful ones adjust for height, so you set how far the screen rises rather than living with one fixed step, and many add a pull-out drawer that turns the dead space under the monitor into somewhere for pens, a notebook or the bits that gather there. It is the pick for a desk that keeps changing, or for anyone who likes to set the rise exactly.

A glass riser is the minimalist's choice: a single clear sheet that recedes into a tidy desk and wipes clean in moments. It carries less and does less, with no storage and a surface that shows every fingerprint, but on a glass-topped or pared-back setup nothing else sits as quietly in the scene.

So look honestly at your own desk. If you mainly want the screen up and the keyboard tucked under, and a bit of warmth in the room suits you, the wooden shelf is the easy call. If the setup is forever being rearranged, or a hidden drawer would earn its keep, lean metal. And if the desk is glass or you keep it deliberately spare, the glass deck respects that better than anything with shelves and legs.

Riser, arm or laptop stand: which is which

These three names get muddled, and buying the wrong one is the usual slip. A riser is the passive desk shelf this guide is about. An arm is the clamp-on, jointed mount that lets a screen float and move, which our riser versus arm comparison weighs in full, VESA and desk-edge checks included. A laptop stand is the odd one out: it props a laptop's own screen to a typing angle rather than lifting a separate monitor, and the best laptop stand guide is the page for that. If your setup is basically right and the screen simply sits too low, the riser is the plain, cheap answer.

Load and stability: match the riser to the screen

The fact the commodity lists skip over is weight. A riser has to hold a screen steady at a raised height, and a shelf that bows under a heavy monitor is worse than no riser at all. The number to read on the listing is the stated load rating, and the sensible move is to leave real headroom over your screen's weight rather than buying right to the limit. The wooden pick here is rated to 30 kg, far more than any single desktop monitor weighs, so it stays steady under a big panel. The metal and glass picks are sized for a normal single monitor; the honest rule is to check a riser's rated load against your screen's weight, which the monitor's own spec sheet lists, before you buy. Footprint matters next to weight: a riser narrower than your monitor's base leaves the stand hanging off the edge, so measure the foot of your screen against the platform, not only the kilograms.

Storage: a drawer, a shelf, or nothing

What a riser does with the space underneath genuinely separates the three, and it is worth weighing before you choose. The wooden pick stacks two tiers, so it lifts the screen and gives the clutter somewhere to sit in one move. The metal pick goes further with a pull-out drawer, which hides pens and oddments out of sight rather than parking them on an open shelf. The glass pick does nothing here by design: it lifts the screen and leaves the desk beneath as tidy or as cluttered as you keep it. If clawing back desk space is half of why you want a riser, that steers you to the wooden shelf or the metal drawer; if you only want the lift and a clean surface, the glass is honest about doing that one thing.

Does a riser get the screen to a better height?

It is worth being careful here, since risers are easy to oversell. A riser lifts a monitor by a fixed amount set by its design, a few inches in practice, which nudges the screen up towards the sort of height many people choose for a monitor, where the top of the screen sits roughly level with the eyeline of someone sitting upright. Whether that lands right for you turns on your chair, your desk height and how tall you are, which is exactly why the adjustable metal pick exists: it lets you set the rise rather than accept one fixed step. We will not oversell what a riser does, because where a screen should sit comes down to your own setup and comfort, not to anything the shelf itself can promise. What a riser gives you is the height, plainly and cheaply; our monitor height explainer covers how people commonly position a screen if you want a mark to aim at.
