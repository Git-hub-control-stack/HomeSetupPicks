What A Standing Desk Keypad Actually Does

Written for the UK • Plain English • No sponsored placements
Written and checked by Home Setup Picks · last updated November 2026
✓ Plain-English explainer ✓ Written for the UK ✓ No sponsored placements

The short answerA standing desk keypad usually carries an up and a down button, a small height display, and a row of numbered memory buttons you set yourself. You save a preset by driving the desk to a height and pressing the set or M button then a number, so one press returns it to that exact height later. Most powered frames also sense a sudden resistance through the motor and back off to limit damage, a feature called anti-collision that you can often tune for how readily it trips.

On a powered standing desk the keypad is the part you touch every day, and on all but the cheapest frames it does a good deal more than send the top up and down. The spread runs from a bare two-button rocker, through a keypad with a handful of saved-height buttons, to a lit panel that hides a whole settings menu. Knowing what the buttons in front of you do, and what sits behind them, is the difference between a desk you nudge into place by eye each time and one that drops to the right height on a single press. This page walks the controller itself: the keys, how you save a height, and the collision sensor that most listings mention and few explain.

The keys in front of you

Almost every handset shares the same core layout. Two arrows raise and lower the top, a small screen reads the current height, and a memory keypad adds a row of numbered buttons, usually four, with a set or "M" key for programming them. The display reads in centimetres or inches, and that unit is switchable in the menu on desks that have one, so a listing quoting inches is not a sign the desk cannot show centimetres. Cheaper frames stop at the up and down rocker with no memory at all, which works but leaves you holding the button and watching the number every time you change posture. The step up to a memory keypad is the one most people feel day to day, because it turns a height change into a single press rather than a hold-and-watch.

How you save a height, and how it comes back

Programming a preset is the same short routine on most desks. You drive the top to the height you want with the up and down buttons, then press the set or "M" key followed by one of the numbered buttons, and that number now belongs to that height. Fully's Jarvis manual puts it plainly: "press the M icon followed by one of the four numbers you want to preset." Some FlexiSpot handsets skip the M key and have you hold the number itself for a few seconds instead, so the sheet in the box settles which routine yours uses. For why those saved buttons earn their keep, our standing desk guide makes the case pick by pick, and for finding your sitting and standing heights in the first place, standing desk height explained walks through it, so this page leaves those two calls there.

Recall comes in two flavours and the difference catches people out. On a one-touch desk you tap the number and the top drives itself to the saved height. On a constant-touch desk you have to hold the button down the whole way, and it stops the moment you let go. The hold is not a fault; it is a safety choice, so a stray elbow or a curious child cannot set a heavy top moving across the room on its own. Many desks let you pick between the two modes in the menu, and some ship locked to constant-touch by design.

What anti-collision really does

Anti-collision is the feature listings name most and describe least. The control box watches the motors as they drive, and when the top meets something solid, a drawer left open, a windowsill, a chair arm, a knee, a taut cable, the sudden jump in resistance registers as a hit and the desk stops and backs off a little. The Jarvis manual describes the hardware behind it: the desk "is outfitted with an accelerometer that stops it if it runs into something," and adds that "you can set the sensitivity with the anti-collision options." Other makers expose the same dial in their own words, FlexiSpot steps it through named levels from off up to high and UPLIFT has you cycle the settings from the handset, and the trade-off is always the same. Set it too keen and a cold, stiff frame on the first move of the morning can read its own tightness as a collision and stop for no reason; set it too slack and the top leans on whatever it has met a little harder before it gives up.

The part worth being honest about is that the sensor reacts after contact, not before. It limits damage; it does not prevent the bump, because it only knows something is there once the top has already pressed into it. That is why every maker, Jarvis included, tells you to keep the path clear rather than trust the sensor to save a full mug or an open drawer. Treat it as a backstop for the thing you forgot, not a licence to leave clutter under the desk. It is also not universal: plenty of budget frames leave anti-collision out entirely or use a single cruder sensor, so if it matters to you it is a spec to check rather than assume.

The settings most people never open

On a lit keypad, holding the set key for a few seconds opens a menu that quietly fixes a lot of small annoyances. You can cap the maximum height so the top never meets a shelf above the desk, set a minimum so it never traps a bin or a drawer below, switch the display between centimetres and inches, lock the keypad so the buttons ignore every press until you switch the lock off, and dim the panel if it glares in a dark room. One option earns its place more than the rest: height calibration. If the number on the display drifts away from reality, you lower the desk all the way down, measure from the floor to the top with a tape, and set the readout to match.

There is a related move for the day a desk starts behaving oddly, flashing an error code or lifting unevenly at one end. Holding the down button until the top bottoms out and then keeps pressing takes the frame through a reset, where it drops slightly, rises slightly and re-syncs its two legs to the same zero. It is the first thing most manuals ask you to try, and it clears the majority of stuck-desk moments on its own, often before support picks up the phone.

FAQ

Q: Why won't my desk move to a saved height unless I hold the button? A: Because it is set to constant-touch mode, where the top only moves while your finger is on the number and stops the instant you let go. It is a deliberate safety design so the desk cannot drive itself across the room from a stray knock. Many desks offer a one-touch mode in the menu instead, where a single tap sends the top to the saved height on its own, though some are locked to constant-touch and cannot be changed.

Q: What does the anti-collision sensitivity setting change? A: How hard a knock it takes to trip the sensor and reverse the desk. A high setting stops at the lightest resistance, which is safest but can false-trip on a cold, stiff frame; a low setting ignores minor resistance, which avoids the false stops but lets the top press harder into a real obstacle first. If your desk keeps halting for no reason, step the sensitivity down a notch; if it feels slow to notice, step it up.

Q: Do all standing desks have anti-collision? A: No. It is common on mid-range and premium frames but often left off cheaper ones, and where it is fitted the quality varies from a single basic sensor to a finer accelerometer. If a collision sensor is on your list, check the spec sheet rather than assume it is there, and compare it against the frames in our standing desk guide. Either way, keep the desk's path clear, because no sensor reacts until after the top has already made contact.

Q: How many memory presets do I actually need? A: Four is the usual count and more than enough for one person: a sitting height, a standing height, and two spare for a second user or a lower height you drop to for focused work. If you share the desk, saving each person's pair is the quickest way to stop the daily nudging over who had it where.
