Gas Lift Class 3 Or Class 4 For Your Office Chair

Written for the UK • Plain English • No sponsored placements
Written and checked by Home Setup Picks · last updated October 2026

The short answerFor most home offices a Class 3 gas lift is the right call, and a Class 4 is usually more cylinder than a single desk needs. The class is a durability grade of the gas cylinder's build rather than a strict weight limit, so Class 4 earns its money on a shared seat, under all-day use or a heavier sitter. If a chair you already have has started sinking, that is a worn cylinder, a cheap part you can swap yourself rather than a reason to replace the whole chair.

What the class actually rates

Spin a task chair over and the part doing the quiet work is the gas lift, the shiny cylinder between the seat and the base that lets the chair rise and drop. The class that goes with it, Class 3 or Class 4, is a grade of that cylinder, and it is easy to read as a weight limit. It is not. The chair maker Merryfair, writing about the German standard the trade cites, puts it plainly: "DIN 4550 classes grade cylinders by tube wall thickness and build quality, not just weight capacity," and "Class is not a strict weight limit. It's a durability grade." So a higher class means a thicker-walled, better-built cylinder meant to take more and last longer, not a chair that holds a different kind of person. Most task chairs you will meet at home carry a Class 3; Class 4 is the step up, with a Class 4 cylinder in a decent chair built to see out the chair's working life of roughly seven to ten years.

Class 3 or Class 4 for a home office

So which do you actually buy? For the typical home setup, the answer is the one most chairs already come with. A single person at a single desk sits well inside what a Class 3 cylinder is built to handle, so you are not settling for less by taking it, and paying up to Class 4 buys headroom you will not use. Class 4 starts to earn its price when the work is harder than that: a seat shared across a shift, a chair in use all day every day, or a sitter at the heavier end who wants the margin, which is why it shows up on executive and round-the-clock chairs. There is one fair reason to choose Class 4 even on your own. The cylinder is a part that wears with use, so fitting the sturdier one is a way to put off the day the seat starts to sink. What Class 4 does not buy is comfort: no class changes how a chair is shaped to you, which is the work of the seat, the backrest and the arms, not the cylinder beneath them.

The number that matters is on your chair, not in a chart

Look the class up and you meet a wall of weight figures, a confident kilo rating for Class 3 and a bigger one for Class 4. Treat them with care. They come from the parts sellers rather than from one published figure, and they do not agree with one another, which is exactly what you would expect of marketing numbers dressed up as a standard. The maker's own point is the one that holds: the class grades build quality, not a weight ceiling. The figure safe to act on is the one the chair itself prints, the weight rating on its listing, because that is a real, maker-stated number you can hold against your own. A sinking seat is a dead cylinder, not a dead chair

If a chair you already have starts to sink, drifting down under you however you set it, the class matters here too, now for the fix. Merryfair describes what has gone wrong without any drama: "your chair sinks because its pneumatic cylinder can no longer hold pressure," a sealed column of compressed nitrogen whose seal has worn so that "nitrogen bleeds past the piston and the chair drops under load." The cure is rarely a new chair. The cylinder is a standard swappable part, and the maker notes that "replacing a cylinder needs a pipe wrench, a rubber mallet, and 15 minutes," with "no screws or clips" in the way. This is the moment the class is worth a thought, because fitting a Class 4 on the way back puts the next sink further off than a like-for-like Class 3 would. Setting the chair's height is the cylinder's everyday job rather than its durability, and that side is a small skill of its own, covered in our guide to what height a chair should be.

FAQ

Q: How do I tell what class my chair's gas lift is? A: Look at the chair's listing or spec sheet first. A maker that has fitted a Class 4 tends to say so, because it is a selling point, so silence usually points to the standard Class 3. The class is not something you can read off the fitted cylinder without taking it out, so if it is not stated and the chair is new, assume the default grade and judge the chair on the weight rating it does publish.

Q: Does any replacement cylinder fit any chair, or do I need to match it? A: Replacement cylinders are sold widely and most task chairs take one without fuss, but the fittings are not identical across every chair. The safe move before you order is to take the old cylinder out and match its diameter and length against the replacement, rather than trust a listing that says it fits all chairs. Get the size right and the swap itself is quick.

Q: Does a higher gas lift class make a chair more comfortable? A: No. The class rates how much load the cylinder carries and how long it is built to last, not how the chair feels. Comfort comes from the seat and backrest, the padding and how well the armrests and recline adjust to you, so a Class 4 cylinder under a basic seat is still a basic seat. Pick the class for durability and judge comfort separately on the parts you sit against.
