Standing Desk Converter Or Full Standing Desk

Written for the UK • Plain English • No sponsored placements
Written and checked by Home Setup Picks · last updated November 2026
✓ Plain-English explainer ✓ Written for the UK ✓ No sponsored placements

The short answerChoose a sit-stand converter if you want to keep the desk you already have, spend less, and test whether a standing routine sticks before committing: it is a riser that sits on top of your current desk, lifts your screen and keyboard together, and needs no building beyond lifting it into place. Choose a full standing desk if you want one clean surface that stays set at every height, more room and weight headroom for a second monitor, and a steadier feel when you type standing, and you are ready to replace the desk itself. The converter is the lower-cost, lower-commitment start; the full desk is the steadier, roomier long-term setup.

What a sit-stand converter is, and where it stops

A sit-stand converter is a riser you set on top of the desk you already own. You rest your screen and keyboard on it, and it lifts them both together a set distance above the desktop so you can work standing, then lowers them again to sit. Nothing is fixed to the desk and nothing replaces it: most arrive ready to use, so the whole job is lifting the unit into place on a desk you keep. That is the appeal. If you have a desk you like, an awkward corner or fitted top you could not cheaply swap out, or you simply do not want to buy a whole new desk to try standing for an hour a day, a converter gets you up on your feet without parting with the desk underneath.

What a converter cannot do is vanish into the desk. It occupies part of the surface, so it takes a bite out of your usable area and brings its own bulk, and because its lift begins at your desktop rather than the floor, even its lowered position adds your desk's own height underneath. The desk maker VersaDesk notes that a converter also carries far less than a full desk does, putting a typical unit at roughly 20 to 35 pounds (around 9 to 16 kg) against 150 pounds or more spread across a full desk's surface, so a heavy screen on a monitor arm can run past what the riser was built to hold. If you want to work out whether your kit is near that line, our guide to standing desk weight capacity shows how to add your load up. VersaDesk makes one more honest point: a converter "can still feel shaky in daily use if the lift mechanism itself is not solid", a thin unit more than a well-braced one.

What a full standing desk changes

A full standing desk takes the opposite route. Rather than stack a lift on your desk, it replaces the desk with a motorised frame that raises the entire top, and that single change is what drives the rest. Because the whole surface travels, your monitor, keyboard, notes and mug all rise together and stay exactly where you left them at any height, on one unbroken top with nothing perched on it. You get the desk's full area and its full weight rating in place of a converter's smaller tray, which is what makes a second screen or a monitor arm an easy fit. A frame standing on the floor also holds steadier under heavy typing at full height than a riser does on a desktop. The cost of all that is real: you are buying and building a new desk and giving it the floor space, where a converter let you keep the desk you had. How to choose for your room

Start with what you are willing to change. If you want to keep a desk you already own, spend the least, or simply find out whether a standing routine will stick before committing to a new desk, a converter is the lower-commitment start, and it suits a single-screen setup that does not ask much of the surface or the weight rating. If you want one top that stays set at every height, room and headroom for a second monitor, and a firmer feel on your feet, and you are set on swapping the desk out, the full desk is the one to reach for. Measure before either way. A converter has to leave you usable depth once its base is down, and a full desk has to clear the room on its feet, both of which our guide to how deep a home office desk should be turns into real numbers. Whichever you land on, set the working height to your own body rather than a figure off the box, in both the sitting and standing positions, which our guide on what height a standing desk should be walks through.

FAQ

Q: Is a sit-stand converter as good as a full standing desk? A: It depends on what you are weighing. A converter wins on keeping your existing desk, costing less and setting up in minutes, which makes it the easy way to try standing at all. A full desk wins on surface area, weight capacity, a single clean top and a firmer feel at standing height, since the whole desk rises instead of a tray lifting on top of it. Neither is better outright: pick the one that fits your desk, your budget and how sure you already are.

Q: Can I put a converter on any desk? A: Almost any sturdy, flat desk, but check two things first. The converter's base needs to fit your desktop with enough depth left over to work, since it takes up the room it stands in, and the desk has to be solid enough to carry the converter plus everything on it without flexing. A tall desk is the catch: because the converter's lowest setting sits on top of your desk's height, even the dropped position can land higher than you want, so measure your seated elbow height against the desk plus the converter's minimum.

Q: Will a converter wobble when I raise it? A: It can, more than a full desk, because a riser perched on a desktop has more give than a frame planted on the floor. As VersaDesk puts it, a converter can still feel shaky if its lift mechanism is not solid, so a heavier, better-braced unit holds steadier than a flimsy one, and a tall, heavy screen makes any converter feel less planted. A full standing desk deals with less of this, which our small home office desk guide covers where it weighs how steady a desk stays at full height.

Q: Should I buy a converter first and a full desk later? A: That is a sensible order if you are not sure standing will suit you. A converter is the cheaper way to find out whether you really do stand once the novelty fades, and if you do, you can move up to a full desk knowing the habit held. If you already know you want to stand and you are committed to a new desk anyway, buying the full one once saves paying twice.
