The real decision on a laptop stand is not the brand, it is a fork: a fixed riser or an adjustable one. A fixed riser is a single rigid shape that sets the screen at one height and never moves again. An adjustable stand trades some of that rigidity for a range of heights and, usually, a folding hinge for travel. For a desk you sit at all day, the fixed riser is almost always the right answer, and it is the one this guide leans on. Fixed or adjustable

The choice between the two is really a choice about how your day works. A fixed riser wins when your laptop lives on one desk: it sets the screen once, holds it dead still, and gives you nothing to fiddle with or knock out of position. An adjustable stand earns its keep when the height needs to change - when two people share the desk, when you alternate between typing on the laptop's own keyboard and pushing it back to reach an external one, or when the stand has to fold flat and travel. The trade is rigidity: a hinge that moves is a hinge that can flex or slip under a heavier laptop, and the folding travel stands in particular are steadier as a perch for reading than as a full-time typing base. If your honest answer is that it sits on this desk and stays there, buy the fixed riser and do not think about it again.

Get the height right

A stand is only half the job; where it puts the screen is the other half. Aim to get the top of the screen roughly level with your eyes when you are sitting up straight, so your eyes meet the screen instead of dropping to a low lid. A riser gets a laptop most of the way there on its own. The part it cannot solve is the keyboard: once the screen is up at eye level the built-in keyboard rises with it and is no longer at a comfortable height to type on, so a separate keyboard and mouse are the natural next buy. And if you run the laptop alongside an external monitor, get both to the same height - our monitor arm guide covers lifting the second screen, and a stand like these handles the laptop. On a sit-stand desk it matters twice over, since the screen has to land at eye level whether the desk is up or down; our standing desk height guide covers getting the desk there first.

FAQ

Q: Do I actually need a laptop stand, or is a stack of books fine? A: A stack of books does the same physical job - it lifts the screen. What a stand adds is stability, airflow underneath, and not having a tower of books slide around. If you only want the screen up for the odd call, books will do; for a desk you use all day, a rigid riser is steadier and lets air move around the machine. How much that airflow really does for heat is its own question - does a laptop stand help with cooling covers what a stand can and cannot do.

Q: Is a fixed riser or an adjustable stand better for a home office? A: For a desk you sit at every day, a fixed riser is usually the better buy: it is more rigid and has nothing to slip. An adjustable stand is worth it when the height genuinely needs to change - a shared desk, or a stand that folds up to travel. Pick the fixed one unless you can name the reason you need movement.

Q: Will these raise my screen enough on their own? A: A riser lifts the screen a good deal - the mStand's own figure is 150mm - but enough depends on your chair and desk height. The target is the top of the screen near eye level. If a riser alone still leaves you looking down, that is usually a sign the laptop needs an external monitor at the right height rather than a taller stand.

Q: Can I use a laptop stand with an external monitor? A: Yes, and it is a common setup. The stand lifts the laptop to act as a second screen, and you match it to the height of your main monitor so the two line up. A monitor arm is the easiest way to get the main screen to the same level.

Q: Are aluminium stands better than plastic ones? A: For holding a laptop steady, aluminium has the edge: it is more rigid, so it does not flex or bow under the weight, and it carries heat away from the machine rather than trapping it. Plastic stands can be fine for light use, but under a heavier laptop they are the ones that sag or creep. Between two otherwise similar stands, the metal one is usually the safer pick.
