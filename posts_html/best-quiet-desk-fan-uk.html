A desk fan earns its place in a home office only if you can forget it is on. The moment it rattles or drones it starts competing with your own thoughts, and it turns up on every call as a wash of background hiss. So the first spec that matters is not how much air a fan shifts but how little of itself you can hear while it does it. The catch is that quiet and powerful tend to pull against each other. A fan gentle enough to sit beside your keyboard rarely moves much air past it, and the one that keeps a whole room comfortable is often the one you reach to switch off when someone dials in. The two fans below sit at the two ends of that trade-off: one built to be nearly silent on a small desk, and one built to keep a room moving without getting loud. Both makers publish their own noise and airflow figures, and where that is all there is to go on, this guide says so rather than dressing a marketing number up as a measurement.

How to buy a quiet desk fan

  1. Read the decibel figure with its setting in mind. Makers quote the low number, and it is almost always the reading on the slowest speed. A fan rated at 28 or 35 decibels is quiet on its gentle setting; wind any fan up to full and it gets louder. Take the quoted figure as the floor it can reach, not the level it runs at all day.
  2. Match the reach to the job before anything else. A personal air circulator like the Dreo aims a focused stream at one seat, while a room fan like the Woozoo oscillates to keep a wider area moving. Decide whether you are cooling yourself or the whole room, because the two fans here are built for different answers.
  3. Oscillation and a remote are the two features worth paying for. Oscillation spreads the air instead of parking it on one spot, so you are not sitting in one unbroken draught for hours. A remote sounds like a frill until you are mid-call and do not want to duck under the desk to turn the speed down.
  4. A DC motor and a longer list of speeds help, where a maker actually lists them. Both tend to mean quieter running and finer control over the breeze. Neither listing here spells the motor type out, so we have not claimed it; check the spec box on the day rather than assuming it.

If it is the laptop itself that runs hot rather than you, a fan is the wrong tool and a cooler is the right one. Our laptop cooling pad guide covers that side.

FAQ

Q: What counts as a quiet desk fan? A: As a rough guide, anything the maker rates around the low 30s of decibels or below is quiet enough to sit beside you on a call, and the two here are rated at 28 and 35 decibels on their gentle settings. Remember that figure is the floor: every fan gets louder as you turn the speed up, so the quiet rating tells you how calm it can be, not how it sounds flat out.

Q: Which of these two should I buy? A: If you want to cool the room and keep air moving while you work, the oscillating Iris Ohyama Woozoo is the all-round choice. If your desk is tight or you mainly want a near-silent breeze on yourself during calls, the smaller Dreo is the cheaper, quieter answer.

Q: Is an air circulator different from a normal desk fan? A: A little. An air circulator, like the Dreo, is shaped to throw a tighter, further-reaching column of air rather than a soft nearby breeze, so it reaches you from a bit further back. For a desk that mostly means you can set it to one side instead of square in front of your face.

Q: Will a desk fan help me stay cool on video calls? A: It can keep the air around you moving, which is the comfort most home workers are after in summer. Point an oscillating fan so it sweeps past you rather than straight at a microphone, and keep it on a low speed on calls so the other side does not hear it.
